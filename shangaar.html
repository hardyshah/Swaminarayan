    આ કેવી રીતે કામ કરે છેHow the analyser works, and how accurate it is

    Why a website cannot simply “see”

    This site is a set of static pages with no server of its own, so the page itself cannot run a big vision model. We give you three ways instead, and we are open about how good each one is.

    1. On this device (default)

    1. Find the face. Murtis have large, bright white almond eyes. The page looks for two white shapes of the same size, side by side, each with a dark pupil, and builds the face box from them. If that fails it uses the detector's own face guess, and you can always correct it.
    2. Look for each item. An open-vocabulary object detector, OWLv2 (Google Research), runs inside your browser through Transformers.js. We ask it about 40 plain-English descriptions such as “turban”, “golden crown”, “earring”, “flower garland”, “peacock feather” and “flute”, and it returns boxes with scores.
    3. Keep only what makes sense. Each item must sit where it belongs on a murti: kundal beside the face at ear height, haar below the chin, kalgi above the forehead, zanzar near the feet. Every murti wears one head-dress, so the page always marks one there and lets the detector vote pagh, mugat or topi. Repeats are merged, and each item has a sensible maximum (two kundal, one bansuri).
    4. You check it. Every mark has a number on the photo and a line in the list. Rename it, remove it, or tap “Add a mark” to add what was missed. The total updates as you go.

    How accurate is it? We tested it on 21 darshan photos shared in this project (Kalupur, Vadtal, Gadhada, Bhuj and others) and checked every mark by eye:

    What we measuredResult
    Face found automatically20 of 21 photos (eyes found in 13, detector guess in 7). Fair-coloured murtis and side views are harder.
    Head-dress marked20 of 21 photos
    Pagh vs mugat vs topi named correctly13 of 20 (65%). Gold zari pagh is often taken for a mugat.
    Marks that were right (6 photos checked mark by mark)About 6 in 10
    Visible items it missedAbout 1 in 4, most often tilak, kundal and the second tora

    So treat the on-device result as a quick first draft. With a minute of correcting, the count is exact.

    2. Expert mode (Claude, your own key)

    The photo is sent from your browser straight to Anthropic's Claude vision model with a list of shangaar names in Gujarati and English, and Claude returns each item with a box. This understands context far better (it can tell a zari pagh from a mugat, count separate haar, and name a makar kundal), but its boxes are approximate and it can still miss tiny pieces. We have not measured it on our test photos, so check its marks the same way. It needs an Anthropic API key from console.anthropic.com; the site never sees or keeps your key.

    3. Mark by hand

    Tap the photo where each item is and pick its name. Nothing is downloaded or sent anywhere.

    “Exact” counting of every bead or flower is not attempted: a garland counts as one item, a pair of kundal as two. Please share photos where it goes wrong, and we will keep tuning it.
